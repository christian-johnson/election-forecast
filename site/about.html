The model in brief

  • Every poll goes into one fit. Generic ballot polls measure the national mood. A poll of a single race measures the national mood plus how far that race leans from it. Fitting them together lets a Michigan poll inform Wisconsin, and a burst of national polls move every race at once.
  • The national mood drifts. It can move a little each week, so recent polls count more than old ones, and the forecast for election day is less certain than the estimate for today.
  • Each race starts from its partisan lean (Cook PVI) plus an incumbency bonus. Its polls then pull it toward what voters say, which captures candidate quality. Races with no polls stay close to where similar races sit.
  • Pollsters differ. Comparing pollsters on the same races and dates reveals each one's consistent lean. The model also adjusts for likely-voter versus registered-voter samples and for polls paid for by a party.
  • Crosstabs move races by who lives there. When a group (say, Hispanic or non-college voters) swings differently from the nation since 2024, each state and district moves in proportion to its share of that group, using Census data.
  • Polls can all miss together. No amount of polling reveals that, so each simulated election adds a national miss, a miss shared within each state, a miss within demographic groups, and a miss for each race. All ranges shown are 80% ranges.
  • Races where one party has no candidate are called for the other side outright.
  • On the forecast page, click any state, district or table row to see that race's forecast built up one step at a time, from the national polls alone to the final number.

A test with a known answer

To check that the method works, we made up a state with five House districts and a Senate seat and decided the truth ourselves: the national mood (Democrats +4 on election day), every race's result, and four pollsters with known leans. We then drew 50 national and 21 race polls from that truth with ordinary sampling noise. Two districts get no polls at all. In two of the versions below, every poll is also shifted by the same industry-wide miss. The model sees only the polls, and is the same code that runs the real forecast.

Limitations

  • The size of a possible shared polling miss is a judgment call, not yet checked against past elections.
  • Georgia runoffs and ranked-choice voting in Maine and Alaska are not modeled; those races are treated as plain two-way contests.
  • Each group's 2024 vote comes from Pew Research Center's validated-voter study. Pollsters' own crosstabs may differ from it, and the model would read that difference as a swing.
  • Census data still describes the old district lines, so districts redrawn for 2026 use their state's demographics instead.
  • Candidates and partisan lean are read from Wikipedia, so a change in its page layout can break the daily update.
