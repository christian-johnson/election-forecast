2026 Midterm Forecast

By Christian Johnson

This is an election forecast model that aims to give reasonable posteriors for the 2026 midterms. It's based on public polling data - no "fundamentals" like the state of the economy, presidential approval ratings, and so on. Which is not to say those aren't useful pieces of information, but dealing with them properly requires some care. My sense is that the large systematic uncertainties associated with polling drown out any additional accuracy one might get from those numbers.

A note for transparency: this application & its code are AI-generated (albeit with a lot of back-and-forth prompting). However, all the prose & explanations you see are human-written.

The methodology of the model is presented via the interactive shown below. Although I show it as a series of discrete steps for explanatory purposes, I want to make clear that the actual fit is not a series of discrete steps. It's a single joint fit across all the pieces of data simultaneously. The reason for that is I wanted to minimize the places where model bias could creep in. By allowing pollster house effects to float along with the rest of the parameters, for example, I don't have to make any assumptions that, say, Republican-affiliated pollsters will produce Republican-biased polls. That may (or may not!) be true. The model is agnostic about those kinds of priors, though.

Methodology

Jump to interactive explanation

The model is built by writing down a likelihood function - something that is maximized when our model fits the data well. Remember, the data we're considering here is just from polls. Each poll gives us a noisy estimate at a point in time of the margin of a particular race, i.e. a measurement of: \begin{equation} M_r = n_E + \lambda_r + \epsilon \end{equation} Where $M_r$ is the margin in race $r$, $n_E$ is the national environment (like D+5, or R+3) on Election Day, $\lambda_r$ is the partisan lean of race $r$, and $\epsilon$ is the noise from a global polling error.

Polling errors, of course, can't be estimated from the polls themselves (that's the whole point of a polling error). Plenty of ink has been spilled about polling errors, though, and we have okay estimates of how big they've been in previous years. So $\epsilon$ is a fixed random normal variate, with a mean of zero and a standard deviation of 3 percentage points (see slide 5 in the interactive below).

The national environment changes over time, so $n_E$ is not going to be the same as $n_{E-4}$ (4 weeks before the election). Polls of the national environment are linked via a random walk over time, such that $n_t = n_{t-1} + \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma)$, where $\sigma$ is fit from the data. If the uncertainty on each poll were known exactly, this would just be a Kalman filter model, but the uncertainties (below) actually have to be fitted. The nice part about a random walk is that it allows for newer polls to count for more without fitting or assuming any decay parameter.

Now that we have got a handle on all the pieces of the margin $M_r$, let's consider the "noisy estimate" part of the poll. The noise comes from a few pieces:

  • Global polling error ($\epsilon$). We covered this above. Note that this doesn't appear in the likelihood itself, we add it post-hoc.
  • Pollster $p$'s house effect ($h_p$): how much a given pollster has partisan bias to its polls. This isn't really "noise" per se - it's a fitted parameter - but it's something taken into account (see slide 4 below).
  • Statistical (sampling) error. This is easy to model: most polls report their sample size. (If they don't, we assume it is 600 people.)
  • Election Day misses for a given race ($\delta_r$). This is informed by an archive of polls from FiveThirtyEight, where we compare the final polls to the actual outcome, after accounting for the national-level polling miss. It's made up of two pieces: polling misses shared by all polls for each individual race, and misses shared by all polls for a given state.

We assume all the noises here are Gaussian. Then we can write down the likelihood function, which is the probability of the data, given the parameters in our model: \begin{equation} L(\theta) = \prod_i \mathcal{N}\left(y_i \;\middle|\; n_{t_i} + \lambda_{r_i} + \delta_{r_i} + h_{p_i},\ v_i + \tau_{o_i}^2\right) \end{equation}

Usually we would maximize $L$ via stochastic gradient descent, or some other optimization method, and then report error bounds on each of the parameters $\theta$. But that's not the goal here. The goal here is to sample the probability distribution of $\theta$ induced by the data. For that, we use a Hamiltonian Monte Carlo method. (Actually, we sample the likelihood multiplied by the prior, which is informed by the initial guess of $\lambda_r$ from the Cook Political Report's PVI). By drawing a large number of samples of $\theta$, such that more likely scenarios are sampled more often, we get a reasonable estimate of the probability of different outcomes. That's what you see in slide 11 in the interactive below.

Keep in mind what you see here doesn't include the covariance estimated from the data and the fit. The resulting trendline you see in the chart is very nearly a Kalman smoother over the data.

Poll noise

Sampling error leads to noise in the poll estimates. That's shown here in the error bars on each bubble, \(v_i\), set by its sample size. When including the sampling noise, the line you see moves just a bit, as you'd expect.

Pollster house effects

House effects \(h_p\) (how much a given pollster's methodology favors Republicans or Democrats) are important to consider. There's one other piece of noise being fit here (\(\tau_o\)). That's a catchall noise term to account for the fact that, even after fitting house effects, different polls of the same race might have wider-than-expected variance, due to differing methodologies. This turns out to be a moderate ( percentage points) contribution to the overall noise estimate.

A global polling error

This is the term that everyone seems to worry about. Polls were famously off in 2016, but they're never perfect, for a variety of reasons (nonresponse bias is the one that folks seem to worry about the most these days). From the FiveThirtyEight archive, we have polling errors going back to 1998 shown here. As you can see, they can be as bad as in , when polls overestimated by percentage points.

Global polling error isn't fit in the model (how could it be?). Rather, for each simulation used in the forecast, we randomly draw a value of \(\epsilon\), such that 80% of the time it is within ± points (the shaded band you see), as of these elections were.

Adding in the polls from the Texas Senate race (and those pesky uncertainties we just talked about), we get a full posterior on the race's lean \(\lambda_r\). The polls are a bit more pro-Talarico than the PVI suggests, so the posterior value of \(\lambda_r\) is a bit above the raw PVI value.

All at once

The real model does not go step by step as we just did: it fits all the parameters across all the data simultaneously. Each simulation draws a different possibility from what fits the data (and from the possible global polling errors), with a probability associated with how well it describes the data. Then we just count how many seats were won by Democrats and Republicans in each simulation, and produce the final histogram of results.
